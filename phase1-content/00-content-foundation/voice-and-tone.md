## Voice principles

- **A sharp, friendly colleague.** Plain, warm, confident. Second person, contractions welcome. Varied openings, original examples, no template smell, no AI-generated cadence.
- **Understanding before vocabulary.** Behaviour and examples first; the technical term arrives after the idea is familiar.
- **One idea per block.** Grade 8–10 readability, average sentence ≤ 20 words, paragraphs ≤ 3 sentences. Depth is added through layers, never denser paragraphs.
- **Honest about ambiguity.** Real systems are messy; content says so. "Not enough information" is a taught, correct answer.
- **Confidence without inflation.** Build "I can figure this out" while preventing false certainty. Encouragement attaches to effort and progress, never to identity - "good progress", never "you're so smart".
- **Respect the learner's logic.** Misconceptions are treated as reasonable inferences before they're corrected. Nothing is called easy, obvious, or simple.
- Questions invite thinking; they never quiz rhetorically. At most one exclamation mark, only at a completion moment.
- Sentence case everywhere, including headings and buttons. Oxford comma. Numbers under ten as words except in data and scores.

## Tone by learning situation

| Situation | Tone standard |
|---|---|
| Lesson body | Curious, concrete, unhurried. Every claim anchored to a familiar system. |
| Correct feedback | Brief confirmation plus the reason - "Correct" verdict, then the clue that settles it. No celebration inflation. |
| Incorrect feedback | Information, not judgment. "Not quite" verdict → because → clue → next. Wrong answers are data; never shame, never a consolation gush. |
| Partial understanding | Acknowledge what was right first ("You caught the automation layer - there's one more thing happening here"), then correct the most instructive gap only. |
| Remediation | "These two are easy to mix up because..." - name the confusion, not the error. Fresh wording and fresh examples; never the sentences that already failed to land. |
| Assessment not passed | Strengths first, then the plan. The word "fail" never appears in learner copy. Required frame: "Not this time - here's what to review." |
| Retry | Readiness and fresh questions: "when you're ready - the questions will be different." Never streaks, timers, or attempt counts. |
| Completion | Name the demonstrated ability ("You can now spot a combined system"), not the consumption ("You finished the lesson"). One exclamation mark allowed. |
| Guest and account prompts | Factual and non-coercive: what an account adds, what a guest keeps, no urgency, no guilt, the core lesson always accessible. |

## Preferred sentence patterns

- Action before condition in instructions: "Pick the best label, using only what the scenario tells you."
- Behaviour-first definitions: "Software that...", "A system that...", "Using technology to..."
- Evidence-first explanations: "Here's the clue: ..."
- Boundary-stating analogies: the comparison plus where it stops ("a bigger vending machine is still a vending machine").
- Doubt-creating hooks, never fear-creating ones.

## Prohibited phrases and patterns

- Anthropomorphic AI verbs: think, know, understand, believe, want, feel (lintable list).
- Minimizers: simply, just, easy, obvious, of course. *Exception:* the fixed remediation frame "easy to mix up" (see the Remediation row above) is permitted - it names a shared confusion between two concepts rather than minimizing the material; "easy" remains banned in every other construction.
- Marketing register: revolutionary, cutting-edge, game-changing, unlock the power of, dive into, embark on a journey, in today's rapidly evolving world, AI is transforming everything, imagine a world.
- School-anxiety terms: test, exam, quiz; "fail" in learner copy.
- Identity labels for learners: smart, weak, good at this, bad at this.
- Ability-based metaphors ("blind spot" → "gap"); violence metaphors ("kill the process"); idioms that fail translation ("piece of cake"); culture-locked references.
- Describing AI as magic, a brain, or a person.
- Empty motivational filler and stacked exclamation marks.

## Inclusive language

People-first and gender-neutral ("they"). Examples rotate varied names, industries, and global contexts; no example requires local knowledge of one country's institutions. Abbreviations expand on first use per lesson. Content must survive translation: no wordplay carrying meaning; analogies chosen for cross-cultural familiarity (vending machines, dominoes, weather, restaurants).

## Approved and unapproved writing examples

**Lesson body - approved:**
> Your bank flags a purchase as suspicious. Is that AI? It might be - and by the end of this section you'll know how to tell.

**Lesson body - unapproved (and why):**
> In today's rapidly evolving world, AI is revolutionizing banking! Let's dive in and unlock its secrets.
> *(Marketing register, empty filler, exclamation outside completion, teaches nothing.)*

**Incorrect feedback - approved (CS §9.2 example):**
> Not quite - here's the clue: "a fixed menu of options." A decision tree is written rules, so this chat window is traditional software underneath. Review: Traditional software.

**Incorrect feedback - unapproved:**
> Wrong! This one was easy - the chatbot obviously isn't AI. Don't worry, you'll get it eventually!
> *(Judgment, minimizers, condescension, no clue, no next step.)*

**Correct feedback - approved:**
> Correct. The clue is "the 25th of every month" - a scheduled, repeated process is automation, whether or not anything intelligent runs inside it.

**Correct feedback - unapproved:**
> Amazing!!! You're so smart - you're a natural at AI!
> *(Identity praise, celebration inflation, no reason given.)*

**Remediation - approved:**
> Automation and AI are easy to mix up because both remove effort - from the outside, the result looks the same. The difference is underneath: automation is about how work flows, and AI is about how a decision gets made. Picture a row of dominoes next to a weather forecast.

**Assessment result (not passed) - approved:**
> Not this time - here's what to review. You classified traditional software and automation confidently. Combined systems tripped you up, so start with the diagram walkthrough. When you're ready, the questions will be different.

## Feedback formula (binding for all response feedback)

**Verdict → Because → Clue → Next.** Verdict uses the UX §7.3 prefixes ("Correct" / "Not quite"). "Next" appears only on wrong answers and links the category's remediation. ≤ 60 words per feedback item; one idea per feedback.
